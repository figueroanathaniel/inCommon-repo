## What a trademark reaches, and what it does not

A trademark reaches **names and source identifiers**: what a thing is called and
who it appears to come from. Copyright reaches **expression**: the particular
words somebody wrote.

Neither reaches a **feature architecture**. Storing a roster of people and
computing the relationship between two of them is a functional idea. "Let users
save other people" is not ownable by anyone, and building it is not a legal
question. What is a legal question is what the feature is called and what text
it prints.

## Cannot do

- Brand any feature as "The Human Design System", or stylise around it.
- Imply affiliation with, or endorsement by, Jovian Archive.
- Use "MyBodyGraph" as a feature name.
- Reproduce Ra Uru Hu's recorded or written teachings, in whole, in paraphrase,
  or as training data.
- Reproduce Jovian's gate and channel interpretation text.
- Claim lineage fidelity: no "trained on the teachings", no "as Ra taught it".

## Can do

- Compute the bodygraph. The inputs are facts and a system: ephemeris
  positions, the I Ching hexagram mapping, the chakra and centre topology.
  Facts are not copyrightable; a system is not copyrightable.
- Describe the mechanics in inCommon's own words.
- Use "human design" lowercase as the generic name of a field of study, the way
  one would write astrology or numerology, rather than as a brand.

## The one that is not settled

**Gate names.** "The Sphinx" and the rest are Ra's naming rather than the I
Ching's, and the study of 27 August recommends avoiding them entirely: a gate is
its number plus inCommon's own short descriptor. This build follows that
recommendation. `hd-atlas.js` carries inCommon's own channel names and
descriptors, and `hd-composite.js` reads its text from there rather than from
anywhere else.

## Counsel gate, still open

Confirm with counsel before shipping the phrase "human design" in store
metadata or in paid acquisition copy. Descriptive fair use is the argument, and
it is an argument rather than a certainty, which makes it exactly the kind of
question that should be answered by a lawyer once rather than by a founder
repeatedly.
